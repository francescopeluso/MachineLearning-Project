\section{Data and evaluation protocol}

\subsection{Annotations and inter-annotator agreement}

The training set has \textbf{707 observations}. The annotations are COCO, with one
structural property that shaped every decision in this project: each entry is
\emph{one annotator's view} of one observation, and the same image appears up to
three times with different polygons (1154 annotator records in total, 8199
annotated filaments). The masks are drawn by hand by human annotators and are
therefore subject to omissions and errors -- this is not a perfect ground truth.
The annotators genuinely disagree: over the 296 observations traced by more than
one person, the mean Dice between two annotators of the \emph{same} image is
$0.62$.

That number measures label noise, not the maximum a model can reach. The score
compares the prediction against \emph{each} annotator separately and then averages,
so a prediction close to the consensus only pays for its own errors, while an
annotator used as a predictor also pays for a colleague's idiosyncrasies. We
measured it: the majority-vote mask of the annotators -- an oracle, built from the
very annotations it is scored against -- reaches a mean Dice of $\mathbf{0.81}$
against the individual annotators. The practical ceiling is there, not at $0.62$:
the delivered model reaches $0.68$ (Section~\ref{sec:results}), above the agreement
between two humans but still $\approx 0.13$ short, a gap that
Section~\ref{sec:error} attributes to recognition of small filaments rather than to
boundary precision.

Filaments are also small and rare (Figure~\ref{fig:dataset}): the median instance
covers $\approx$1230 pixels and all of them together only $0.4\%$ of the image,
which rules out a plain cross-entropy loss and makes input resolution a
first-class variable.

\subsection{Split protocol}

The split is on \textbf{file names, never on annotation records}: two annotators of
one observation describe the same pixels, and splitting on records would leak
validation pixels into training and inflate every score. Seed 22 divides the 707
observations into three blocks of 494, 106 and 107, written to
\texttt{splits/split\_seed22.csv}. The third one was an internal test set, never
consulted to choose anything; from the seventh experiment on we dropped the
three-way split and folded it into training, where 107 observations are worth more.
The protocol actually used, and the one behind the delivered model, is therefore
two-way: \textbf{601 training and 106 validation observations}, with the validation
set as the only instrument for selecting hyperparameters, checkpoints and the
operating point.

\subsection{The official metric, re-implemented}

The project work assignment adopts the \textbf{Mean Dice} as its evaluation score;
the Kaggle challenge scores its leaderboard with \textbf{Panoptic Quality}, so we
track and report both. The challenge's PQ differs from the textbook definition of
Section~\ref{sec:background} in three ways that all change what a good prediction
looks like: ground truth is one instance per annotation rather than connected
components of the union of every annotator's polygons; each annotator is a separate
evaluation entry, so one prediction is scored two or three times against experts
who disagree with each other; and TP, FP and FN accumulate globally over the split
instead of being averaged per image. The pipeline re-implements exactly that
definition and verifies it against the organisers' published code, agreeing to
$3\times10^{-8}$.
