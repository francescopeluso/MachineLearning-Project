\section{The task, the data and the metrics}
\label{sec:framing}

Solar filaments are ribbons of cooler plasma suspended over the solar disk, visible as
dark, elongated structures in H-alpha images. The task is binary segmentation with an
instance twist: given a $2048 \times 2048$ observation, predict which pixels belong to
a filament, split the mask into one connected component per filament, and submit each
component as a COCO run-length encoding.

\subsection{What the dataset looks like}
\label{sec:dataset}

The training set contains \textbf{707 observations} from the six stations of the GONG
network (Big Bear, Cerro Tololo, Learmonth, Mauna Loa, El Teide, Udaipur), taken
between 2011 and 2022 with a strong skew towards the 2011--2015 solar maximum. The
annotations come from the MAGFiLO catalogue and carry a structure that shaped the whole
project: each of the \textbf{1154 annotation records} is \emph{one annotator's view} of
one observation. 411 files were annotated by a single expert, 145 by two and 151 by
three, and the annotators genuinely disagree -- the mean Dice between two annotators of
the \emph{same} image is $0.62$ (median $0.65$).

The 8199 annotated filaments are small and thin: the median filament covers about
1230~pixels at native resolution (interquartile range 670--2430, 15\% below 500), and
all filaments together cover only $\approx 0.4\%$ of the pixels. Three consequences
drive the design. The extreme class imbalance rules out a plain cross-entropy loss;
structures a few pixels wide are destroyed by aggressive downscaling, which will make
\emph{resolution} the most productive variable of the study; and with only 494 training
observations (the training set is explicitly not extendable), transfer learning and
augmentation are the only remaining levers.

\subsection{Metrics: IoU, Dice, and Panoptic Quality}
\label{sec:metrics}

Two masks $P$ (prediction) and $G$ (ground truth) can be compared by their
\textbf{Intersection over Union} or by the closely related \textbf{Dice coefficient}:
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IoU}(P,G) = \frac{|P \cap G|}{|P \cup G|},
\qquad
\mathrm{Dice}(P,G) = \frac{2\,|P \cap G|}{|P| + |G|}.
\end{equation}
%
Both are pixel-level measures. Dice is what the course grades; the Kaggle submission,
however, asks for one row per \emph{filament}, and the leaderboard reflects
\textbf{Panoptic Quality}. Predicted and annotated instances are matched when their
IoU exceeds $0.5$ (matched pairs are true positives, unmatched predictions false
positives, unmatched annotations false negatives), and
%
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PQ} = \frac{\sum_{(p,g) \in \mathrm{TP}} \mathrm{IoU}(p,g)}
                   {|\mathrm{TP}| + \tfrac{1}{2}|\mathrm{FP}| + \tfrac{1}{2}|\mathrm{FN}|}
            = \underbrace{\frac{\sum \mathrm{IoU}}{|\mathrm{TP}|}}_{\mathrm{SQ}}
              \times
              \underbrace{\frac{|\mathrm{TP}|}
                               {|\mathrm{TP}| + \tfrac{1}{2}|\mathrm{FP}| + \tfrac{1}{2}|\mathrm{FN}|}}_{\mathrm{RQ}}.
\end{equation}
%
The factorisation matters: \emph{Segmentation Quality} measures how well matched
instances overlap, \emph{Recognition Quality} measures how many instances are found at
all. The gap between Dice and PQ is the whole story of this project -- a filament
broken into three fragments, or a correct mask surrounded by twenty ten-pixel specks,
moves Dice by a fraction of a percent and collapses RQ.

The competition's own PQ, published in the organisers' self-evaluation notebook,
differs from the textbook definition in three ways that all change what a good
prediction looks like: ground truth is \emph{one instance per annotation}, not
connected components of the union of all annotators' polygons; \emph{each annotator is
a separate evaluation entry}, so one prediction is scored two or three times against
experts who disagree; and TP/FP/FN accumulate \emph{globally} over the split rather
than per image. The pipeline re-implements exactly this definition and verifies it
against the reference code (agreement to $3 \times 10^{-8}$), so every validation
number in this report is directly comparable with the leaderboard.

\subsection{How good is good enough}

The inter-annotator Dice of $0.62$ is a practical ceiling: past it, a model is not
segmenting better, it is fitting one expert's notion of where a filament ends. The
number reframes the problem -- from the second experiment onwards the model is already
at that ceiling on Dice while PQ sits around $0.25$, so the headroom is entirely in how
the mask is \emph{organised into instances}. Every hypothesis that follows is built on
that observation.
